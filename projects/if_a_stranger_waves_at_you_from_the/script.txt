If someone waves at you from your backseat tonight, do not pull over.
Do not tap the brakes.
Do not look in your rearview mirror twice.
Keep both hands locked at ten and two.
And whatever you do, do not ask them how they got in.
They did not crawl through your trunk.
They did not jimmy your locks.
You invited them five miles back without even realizing it.
There are seven survival rules for the backseat passenger.
Seven laws to keep your foot on the gas until daybreak.
Rule one through six will keep you alive on Route Nine.
Rule seven will make you wish you ran into a tree.

My name is Julian.
I am a long-haul night courier.
I drive a faded gray sedan with two hundred thousand miles.
The heater rattles like teeth in a jar.
The headlights only cut thirty yards into the fog.
At two seventeen in the morning, Route Nine belongs to nobody.
It is sixty miles of pine barrens and dead radio air.
No gas stations.
No highway patrol.
Just two narrow lanes sliced through pitch black timber.
I had three cardboard boxes sealed with yellow tape in my trunk.
I had half a thermos of black coffee on the console.
And my phone was down to four percent battery.
Then the interior chill hit my neck.

It was not the cold draft of winter.
It was the heavy, sweet cold of an open freezer chest.
The smell came next.
Wet wool, dried copper, and old stagnant pond water.
My dashboard clock flickered once.
The glowing green numbers bled into vertical lines.
Two seventeen became zeros.
Then I felt the seat back flex against my spine.
Someone was pressing their knees directly into the foam behind me.
Slowly.
Deliberately.
Testing the weight of my ribcage through the fabric.

My right foot twitched toward the brake pedal.
Every human instinct screams at you to stop the car.
You want to kick open the door.
You want to bolt into the treeline.
That is the first mistake.
That is how they catch you outside the steel frame.
Inside the car, you have five hundred feet of forward momentum.
Outside the car, you have nothing.

A crumpled piece of receipt paper sat stuffed inside my air vent.
It had been there for three years, left by the old courier who trained me.
He told me never to unfold it unless the mirrors turned gray.
I reached out with trembling fingers.
I pulled the paper free.
Written in faded red grease pencil were seven rules.

Rule One. Never touch the center rearview mirror.
You can use your side mirrors to stay inside your lane.
You can glance at your blind spots with extreme caution.
But the center mirror belongs entirely to the backseat now.
If your eyes meet their eyes in the glass, the contract is signed.
You acknowledge their presence.
And once you acknowledge them, they are allowed to touch your shoulders.

I kept my chin pointed straight at the white lines on the asphalt.
The temptation was a burning wire inside my skull.
My peripheral vision caught a pale blur between the two front bucket seats.
It was low.
Crouched in the center of the bench.
Then, a slow rustle of wet fabric slid against the vinyl seat.
A pale, water-logged hand rose into the dim green wash of the dashboard.
The fingers were impossibly long.
The nails were split, lined with dark forest mud.
The hand did not grab me.
It did not scratch.
It simply raised two knuckles.
And slowly, gently, it waved.
A casual, friendly wave.
Like an old acquaintance passing you on a rainy sidewalk.
My throat closed up.
The knuckles clicked with each tilt of the wrist.
*Click. Click. Click.*
I bit the inside of my cheek until warm blood pooled on my tongue.
Pain kept my eyes forward.
Pain kept my foot heavy on the throttle.

Rule Two. The windows must remain cracked exactly two inches.
Not rolled up tight.
Not rolled down halfway.
Exactly two inches of open glass.
If you seal the cabin, the air pressure drops within three minutes.
The oxygen thins until your lungs burn with sulfur.
If you roll them down too low, whatever is running alongside your tires will reach inside.
I hit the master power switch with my thumb.
The driver window groaned downward.
Cold mountain wind blasted against my cheek, tearing the copper smell away.
Through the two-inch crack, the whistle was deafening.
It sounded like a screaming tea kettle.
Or dozens of voices whispering through a keyhole.
[whisper] "Pull over, Julian. You look so tired."
I did not answer.
I pressed the child lock button on the door armrest.
The red LED indicator flashed on.
You must never let them operate the window switches.

Rule Three. If the radio turns on by itself, do not turn it off.
Adjust the volume, but never hit the power knob.
At two twenty-six, the stereo display clicked bright red.
The static roared through all four speakers like a burst pipe.
White noise rattled the speaker grilles on the door panels.
Then the frequency swept backward through dead channels.
Ninety-one point one.
Eighty-nine point five.
It settled on an empty AM band that had no broadcast license.
A brass big-band tune was playing from a warped vinyl record.
Scratchy, slow, and sickeningly sweet.
Underneath the trumpets, someone was weeping.
A small, choked sob that repeated every four bars of music.
I reached down with a shaking hand.
I gripped the volume dial.
I turned it down until the brass horn was a tiny needle in my ear.
I left the power button untouched.
If you kill the radio, the silence fills with their breathing.
And once you hear their lungs rattle behind your ear, you will turn around.
You will not be able to stop yourself.

Behind me, the passenger shifted their weight.
The vinyl squeaked loudly.
The wet smell surged forward again, overpowering the rush of outside wind.
A cold drop of liquid landed directly on the center console.
It was dark, viscous, and thick like motor oil.
It dripped from the ceiling liner right between my elbow and the gear shift.
I looked up through my eyelashes.
The fabric of the car roof was sagging downward.
Two heavy impressions pressed into the beige cloth above my head.
The shape of two bare knees.
They were not sitting on the bench anymore.
They were perched on the top of the backrest.
Hovering right behind my headrest.

Rule Four. Watch the oncoming headlights.
If a car approaches from the opposite direction, check their high beams.
Out on Route Nine, normal traffic is rare.
If a vehicle approaches with yellow halogen lights, they are human.
You may flash your lights once to stay alert.
If a vehicle approaches with harsh violet or deep crimson beams, close your eyes for three seconds.
Do not swerve.
Lock your steering wheel dead center and count out loud.
At two thirty-four, two pinpricks of violet light pierced the fog ahead.
They were moving fast.
Eighty miles an hour, barreling down the double yellow divider line.
The purple glare reflected off my wet windshield like spilled ink.
My heart slammed against my ribs like a caged crow.
One hundred yards.
Fifty yards.
I clamped my eyelids shut.
One.
The violet light burned through my eyelids, painting the inside of my skull neon purple.
Two.
A wall of freezing air hit the driver door, rocking the sedan on its worn shocks.
A horn blared—not a car horn, but the low, mechanical bellow of an iron factory whistle.
Three.
I snapped my eyes open.
The road was empty.
The black asphalt stretched out ahead, slick with freezing rain.
The rear suspension groaned.
Whatever was crouching behind me had dropped back down onto the floorboards.

Rule Five. Never look at the roadside hitchhikers.
Between mile markers forty and fifty-two, you will see people standing on the shoulder.
They will look familiar.
They will wear the yellow raincoats of your childhood neighbors.
They will wear your mother’s knitted winter scarf.
They will hold gas cans, or broken umbrella frames, or dead cell phones.
They will flag you down with frantic, two-handed waves.
Do not even glance at their faces.
They are not stranded motorists.
They are the passengers who pulled over last week.
They are waiting for your seat to become empty.

At mile marker forty-six, a figure stood beneath a dead oak tree.
The pale yellow raincoat was torn at the hem.
The figure stepped off the gravel shoulder and placed one boot onto the white line.
As my headlights swept across the figure, my stomach dropped into a void.
It was wearing my brother’s blue high school track jacket.
The faded white lettering across the chest read: *RIVERDALE CROSS COUNTRY*.
The figure raised its right arm.
Its hand was blackened by frostbite, fingers curled like dead spider legs.
It waved.
The exact same slow, rhythmic, two-knuckle wave.
*Click. Click. Click.*
Behind me, the backseat passenger mirrored the motion.
I saw the long-fingered shadow rise on the dashboard.
Both of them were waving in perfect synchronization.
A synchronized cadence.
Like two musicians keeping time in an empty theater.
[whisper] "He's cold, Julian. Let your brother in."
The voice came from the footwell behind my driver's seat.
It was dry, raspy, like dry leaves scraping over concrete.
I stared at the odometer.
Forty-seven miles.
Forty-eight miles.
I swallowed the bile rising in my throat and muttered through clenched teeth:
"He died ten years ago in Michigan. You're not him."
The backseat went dead silent.
The floorboards creaked as the weight shifted back toward the passenger side door.

Rule Six. Do not trust your fuel gauge after mile fifty.
The instrument cluster lies to save the entity, not you.
It will tell you the tank is full to keep you driving past your exit.
Or it will plummet to empty to bait you into pulling off onto a dirt logging road.
Trust your trip odometer.
Calculate your mileage in your head.
If you pull into an abandoned gas station before sunrise, the car will never start again.
At two forty-eight, the red fuel light on my dashboard pinged.
*Ding.*
The needle dropped like a severed stone, pinning itself against the empty peg.
The digital display flashed: *RANGE: 0 MILES*.
Right on cue, a rusted neon sign flickered through the pine branches on the right.
*LAST CHANCE PETROLEUM — OPEN 24 HOURS.*
The pumps were rusted skeletons covered in ivy.
The glass in the cashier booth was shattered.
A single halogen light buzzed over pump number two, casting yellow rings on the cracked concrete.
My foot hovered over the brake.
The engine sputtered.
The sedan gave a sharp, violent shudder, as if the fuel line was sucking pure air.
It felt so real.
The vibration traveled right through the soles of my boots.
The steering wheel fought my grip, pulling hard toward the gravel turnoff.
"Just five gallons," a voice purred from the darkness behind me.
It was inches from my right ear.
I could feel the cold vapor of its breath against my neck hairs.
"Pull in, Julian. We can walk the rest of the way together."
I braced my arms against the wheel.
I stomped the accelerator straight down into the floor mat.
The car hesitated for two agonizing seconds.
Then the four-cylinder engine screamed.
The tires bit into the wet highway asphalt.
We blasted past the rusted pumps at seventy miles an hour.
In the split second we passed under the yellow halogen light, I saw the station window.
There was no floor inside the station.
Just a sheer, vertical pit dropping into endless black water.
If I had turned into that lot, the sedan would have plunged straight through the collapsed concrete.

The dashboard fuel needle slowly floated back up to a quarter tank.
The engine smoothed out.
The digital display flickered and went dark.
I checked the trip counter.
Fifty-eight miles.
Only two miles left until the county bridge.
The bridge marked the end of Route Nine.
Concrete spans over rushing river water.
The old courier had told me: *Entities of the barren cannot cross moving water.*
Once your front tires hit the metal expansion joint of the bridge, the back seat clears.
My hands were cramping around the wheel.
My knuckles were white and bloodless.
I could see the orange streetlights of the bridge tower reflecting on the wet clouds ahead.
One mile.
Just one mile.

Then the paper receipt slipped from my fingers and fell into my lap.
The bottom crease had been folded over twice.
A small line of text was scrawled at the very bottom in dark, jagged ink.
Rule Seven.
The rule the old courier told me never to read unless I made it to the river.

Rule Seven. Check who is holding the steering wheel.
My breath caught in my chest.
The paper began to tremble in my lap.
The text continued in hurried, frantic handwriting:
*The entity never enters through the trunk.*
*The entity never climbs through the back window.*
*The entity does not ride in the back.*
*The entity drives.*

My eyes slowly tracked down from the road to my own hands.
Ten and two on the steering wheel.
The fingers were long.
The skin was gray, swollen, and sodden with stagnant river water.
The nails were split, packed with black mud from the bottom of the pond.
I looked at the center rearview mirror.
I broke Rule One.
I looked right into the glass.

Sitting in the backseat of the sedan was a young man in a worn canvas courier jacket.
His face was pale with terror.
His eyes were wide, bloodshot, locked in panic.
His hands were pressed tight against his chest, clutching an old delivery clipboard.
He was staring at the back of my head.
I felt my own jaw unhinge.
I felt my own cold lips pull back over wet, jagged teeth.
My right hand lifted off the steering wheel against my will.
Two knuckles raised into the dim green dashboard light.
And slowly, rhythmically, I waved at him in the mirror.
*Click. Click. Click.*
[whisper] "Welcome to Route Nine, Julian."
The sedan crossed the bridge.
And the car kept driving forever into the dark.
